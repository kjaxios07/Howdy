§ 01

Start here

Howdy is a chat companion called Kip. An international student or new migrant asks a question about visas, tax, housing, work or health, and Kip answers in plain English with a link to the official Australian source it took the answer from. It is a first point of contact — the thing you ask before you know whether you need to pay an expert.

Everything in this document exists to support two promises. The first is accuracy: Kip never invents a fee, a date, a rule or a rental listing, and every answer carries a citation you can click. The second is confidentiality: a student can ask about a visa refusal, an underpaying employer or a mental health crisis, and that question does not become a liability for them.

Those two promises pull in opposite directions, and most of the engineering here is about holding both at once. Accuracy wants us to remember everything students ask so the product improves. Confidentiality wants us to remember nothing. Section 3 explains exactly where we drew that line, and section 10 shows what an attacker would actually walk away with if they got everything.

The line this product lives on

Under the Migration Act 1958, giving immigration assistance without registration is a criminal offence. Providing general information is not. Kip explains what a visa is, what it costs, what it requires and how the process runs — and stops the moment a question turns on one person's own circumstances, at which point it refers to a MARA-registered agent. That distinction is not a disclaimer bolted on at the end; it is designed into the question bank, the system prompt and the answer format. See §13.

§ 02

What an LLM actually is

LLM stands for Large Language Model. Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini are all LLMs. Kip is built on one — specifically Claude — but Kip is not just an LLM, and the difference is the whole product.

The plain version

An LLM is a very large statistical model of written language. It was trained by reading an enormous amount of text and learning, over and over, to predict what word comes next. That is genuinely all it does at the mechanical level: given everything so far, produce the most plausible next piece of text.

That simple mechanism produces something that reads like understanding. It can explain, summarise, translate, follow instructions and hold a conversation. But three consequences follow directly from how it works, and each one is a risk for a product that students will act on:

  • It has no memory between conversations. A fresh LLM call starts with nothing. Anything it "knows" about a user, we had to send it in that request. This is a privacy advantage and we lean on it hard.
  • It does not look things up by default. Its knowledge is frozen at training time. Ask it the current Australian minimum wage and it will answer from memory — which may be two rate rises out of date.
  • It will produce a confident wrong answer. Because it is predicting plausible text, an invented visa fee looks exactly as fluent as a real one. This is usually called hallucination. For a student about to lodge a form, it is the single most dangerous failure mode we have.

So Kip is an LLM on three leashes

We do not ship a raw LLM to students. Every answer passes through three independent controls, and each one would catch a failure the others missed.

Which model, and what that costs

Kip runs on claude-sonnet-5 through Anthropic's API, capped at 700 output tokens per answer. The model is a configuration value (KIP_MODEL), not a hard-coded choice — switching to the cheaper Haiku tier is a one-line change once we have blind-tested answer quality. The system prompt is roughly 17,000 tokens and is byte-identical on every request, so prompt caching applies: after the first call, re-reading that prompt costs about a tenth of a fresh read. That single decision is the difference between viable and unaffordable unit economics at student volumes.

Worth saying out loud

We do not train a model. We do not send student conversations anywhere to be trained on. Anthropic's API does not train on business API traffic. Kip's "learning" (§3) happens in our own database, on de-identified question topics only — never on conversation content.

§ 03

Your 434 questions: where they live and how they come back

You gave me 220 researched questions in a spreadsheet plus your platform document. Those merged with the earlier seed bank into 434 questions, and they are stored in three different places for three different reasons. This section is the direct answer to "where are you going to save the questions I gave you and where will you retrieve them from?"

How a stored question comes back

  • As product structure. The 434 questions define the 13 modules and their real counts — housing 50, visa 45, work 45, and so on. The dashboard tiles a student sees are generated from the bank, not typed by hand.
  • As suggested prompts. When a student opens a module, the starter questions shown are drawn from your bank, filtered to that module.
  • As answer shape. Your spreadsheet's Search Intent column does real work. Informational questions get an explanation; Procedural questions get numbered steps naming the actual form; Investigative questions ("is this a scam?", "am I being underpaid?") get a verdict first, then red flags, then the action. A student who suspects they are being ripped off does not need a balanced overview — they need an answer.
  • As a safety net. The risk tier travels with the question. 12 questions are refer — they always carry a MARA or legal referral. 9 are crisis — they lead with emergency resources before anything else.
  • As the evolution queue. The 65 questions flagged volatile contain a figure that changes — wage rates, visa fees, thresholds. Those are re-checked automatically against their cited source rather than recalled from memory.

Evolving every day, without becoming a liability

You asked for the tool to get better every day. It does — but the daily job is split deliberately in two, because a wrong auto-published fact about work-hour limits could cost a student their visa.

§ 07

How the encryption works

The design assumption is blunt: one day someone will get a copy of the database. A stolen backup, a misconfigured restore, a hosting company subpoena, a disgruntled contractor. The job of this section is to make that copy worthless.

Encrypting the whole disk does not achieve that — full-disk encryption protects a stolen physical drive, and nothing else. A running server has the disk unlocked, so anyone who can read the database can read the data. So the content is encrypted before it reaches the database, with a key the database does not contain.

Three questions your engineer will ask

Q

If the app server can decrypt, hasn't it just moved the problem?

Partly, and that is honest. Full compromise of the running app server does expose data as it flows through. What this design defeats is every attack that isn't that — dumps, backups, restores, read-only SQL injection, snapshot leaks, subpoenas to the hosting provider. Those are the overwhelming majority of real breaches.

Q

How do you find a user by email if email is encrypted?

A separate HMAC of the lowercased address, keyed with a server-side pepper, stored in email_hash with a unique index. Lookups hit the HMAC; the address itself only ever comes back decrypted. Without the pepper the hashes cannot be brute-forced from a dump.

Q

What if the KEK is lost?

Every saved conversation becomes permanently unreadable. That is the correct trade and it must be planned for: the KEK is backed up separately from the data, in a different place, with a different access path. Backup and key must never travel together.

§ 08

Login and sessions

Students sign in with Google. We never see or hold a password, which removes an entire category of breach — there is no password database to steal, no reset flow to abuse, no credential-stuffing surface.

Why the session is a cookie and not a token in the browser

The common shortcut is to put a JWT in localStorage. It is easier to build and it is a bad trade. Any cross-site scripting bug on the page can read localStorage and walk away with a valid login, and a JWT cannot be revoked before it expires — if a student's session is stolen, you cannot end it.

Howdy issues an opaque random session id, stored server-side in Valkey, delivered in a __Host- prefixed cookie that is httpOnly (JavaScript cannot read it), Secure (HTTPS only), SameSite=Lax (not sent on cross-site requests) and locked to the exact origin. Signing a student out, or ending a stolen session, is a single delete on the server and takes effect immediately. The session id is also regenerated at the moment of login, which closes session fixation.

§ 09

The three privacy modes

Earlier the plan was to destroy every session after two minutes. That protected privacy by making the product nearly useless — a student could not come back to the checklist Kip gave them yesterday. This is the replacement: the student chooses, and the strictest option is always one tap away.

Signed out

Guest

Full access to Kip. No account, no cookie beyond the request, nothing written to the database at all. Close the tab and it is genuinely gone.

This is the mode for the student who wants to ask about a visa refusal without it existing anywhere.

Signed in, opted out

Incognito

Signed in, history available from earlier conversations, but this conversation is not written. One toggle in the chat window, on and off mid-session.

For the sensitive question inside an otherwise ordinary relationship with the product.

Signed in, default

Saved

Conversations kept, encrypted per §7, for a retention period the student picks: 30 days, 90 days, a year, or until they delete them.

This is what makes Kip a companion rather than a search box — it remembers the thread of someone's arrival.

The property that makes this credible

In guest and incognito modes, the write path is not merely skipped at the end — the code never constructs a row. There is no "delete it later" step that could fail silently, no temporary buffer, and no log line carrying the content. The only trace a guest conversation leaves in the whole system is a rate-limit counter that expires in seconds and a possible de-identified topic row in question_gaps if Kip answered badly.

§ 11

Cloudflare and the VPS

You said you can do a VPS and "storage for Cloudflare or something you reckon". Here is what I reckon, and why: Cloudflare in front, not underneath. The database stays on your VPS. Cloudflare handles the edge and holds encrypted backups.

JobWhere it goesCostReasoning
DNSCloudflareFreeFast, free, and the control plane for everything below.
TLS + CDNCloudflareFreeStatic files served from an edge close to the student. Caddy still holds a real certificate at the origin so the hop from Cloudflare to your server is encrypted too — set the mode to Full (strict), never Flexible.
DDoS + firewallCloudflareFreeAbsorbs volumetric attacks before they touch a 12 GB box, and hides your origin IP so the VPS cannot be attacked directly.
Bot and abuse controlCloudflare TurnstileFreeBlocks scripted abuse of an expensive endpoint at the edge. Your per-answer model cost makes this worth having on day one.
Encrypted backupsCloudflare R2~$0.02/GB/moNo egress fees, which is the reason to pick it over S3 — restoring a backup should never come with a surprise bill.
Application + databaseYour VPS$0–24/moStays put. See below.

Why the database does not move to Cloudflare

Cloudflare has a database product (D1) and a key-value store, and they are good. They are the wrong choice here for three reasons. The encryption model in §7 depends on holding a master key on a filesystem the database cannot read — a managed serverless database removes that separation. Second, Australian universities and government buyers ask where data is physically stored, and "on a server in Sydney that we control" is a much easier answer than explaining a distributed edge platform. Third, Postgres row-level security is doing real work as the IDOR backstop, and it has no equivalent there.

So: Cloudflare is the front door and the offsite vault. The VPS is the house.

Running cost, honestly

Infrastructure is the small number. At beta scale you are looking at roughly $6–30/month for hosting, ~$15/year for a .au domain, and $0 for Cloudflare. The variable cost that matters is the model: every answer costs tokens, every live search costs more. Prompt caching, the 700-token output cap and the 3-search ceiling exist to hold that line. Measure cost per answer from week one of the beta — it is the number that decides whether any of the models in §16 work.

§ 12

Deletion, retention and the student's rights

Australian privacy law is not the constraint people assume. The Privacy Act's Australian Privacy Principles are mostly common sense written down, and the architecture already satisfies most of them. What matters is that the promises are enforced by code, not by policy.

PrincipleHow Howdy meets it
Collect only what you needNo date of birth, no address, no phone, no visa number, no passport. Name and email arrive from Google; the email is encrypted immediately. Personal details typed into chat are actively refused.
Be open about itGuest mode is offered before sign-in, the incognito toggle sits in the chat window itself, and retention is a setting the student chooses rather than a clause they scroll past.
Keep it secure§7 and §10.
Let people see and correct itExport returns every conversation as readable JSON, decrypted on the way out.
Destroy it when it is no longer neededRetention runs as a scheduled job, not a promise. Expired conversations are deleted by expires_at, and account deletion cascades from the user row through every conversation and message.
Notify a serious breachThe 72-hour notification path needs to be written down before launch — see the warning in §10.

What "delete my account" actually does

  • The users row is deleted, which cascades to every conversation and every message. The ciphertext is gone, not merely orphaned.
  • The wrapped DEK goes with it — so even a backup taken yesterday cannot be decrypted for that student, because their key no longer exists in any live system.
  • Their sessions are dropped from Valkey immediately.
  • The audit_log rows survive with user_id set to null. We keep the fact that a deletion happened, detached from who it happened to — that is the record which proves the deletion ran.
  • question_gaps is untouched, because it was never connected to them in the first place.
§ 13

How we actually serve students and migrants

The architecture exists to support a service model, not the other way round. This is what Howdy does for a person, in the order they need it.

The arrival journey

  1. Before landingWhat to pack, what OSHC is and why it must start before arrival, what a Confirmation of Enrolment does, what the visa conditions actually say. The arrive and cost modules — 40 questions between them.
  2. First fortnightThe bureaucratic sprint: apply for a Tax File Number, open a bank account, get a transport card, find somewhere to live. Almost all Procedural questions, so the answers are numbered steps naming the real form and the real website.
  3. First jobAward rates, the 48-hours-per-fortnight work limit, payslips, superannuation, and the big Investigative one — am I being underpaid? The work and money modules, 85 questions.
  4. Settling inTax returns, Medicare and OSHC claims, tenancy disputes, consumer rights, public transport concessions. This is where a companion beats a search engine: the student does not know the name of the thing they need.
  5. When something goes wrongScams, exploitation, discrimination, mental health crises, family violence. The safety and rights modules. These carry the crisis tier — emergency resources lead, everything else follows.

Three tiers of answer

standard · 413 questions

Answer it

General information from official sources, cited, shaped to the intent. The bulk of the product.

refer · 12 questions

Answer, then hand over

The question turns on someone's own circumstances — PR pathways, visa strategy, a legal dispute. Kip explains what exists, then names a MARA-registered agent or the university's free student legal service. This is the Migration Act line, enforced in data.

crisis · 9 questions

Safety before information

Family violence, mental health emergency, assault. The answer opens with 000 and Lifeline 13 11 14, then the university's support service. Information second.

Why referral is a feature, not a limitation

Every referral is a moment where Howdy proves it is not trying to be the last word. That is what earns institutional trust — and it is also the commercial engine (§14, §16). A tool that knows the edge of its own competence is a tool a university can put its name next to.

§ 14

Adding vendors

A vendor is a real service a student needs after the general information runs out: a MARA-registered migration agent, an accountant, a tenancy advocate, a student health service, a phone or bank provider. Howdy's advantage is that it knows the precise moment someone needs one.

The risk is equally precise. The instant a referral is influenced by who paid, the product stops being trustworthy — and for migration agents specifically, referring a student to an unregistered "agent" is a serious problem regardless of what money changed hands.

The vetting gate

  1. Register or licence checkMigration agents verified against the MARA register by registration number. Lawyers against their state legal board. Accountants against CPA, CA ANZ or IPA. Real estate agents against the state licensing body. No number, no listing.
  2. Insurance and ABNCurrent professional indemnity insurance and a valid ABN, both recorded with their expiry so a lapse triggers a review rather than being discovered by a student.
  3. Written service standardsA committed response time, published fees or fee ranges, and a complaints path. Vendors who will not publish a fee range do not get listed.
  4. Student-facing disclosureEvery listing states plainly that Howdy may be paid for the introduction. Not in a footer — on the card.
  5. Continuous reviewRegistration re-verified on a schedule. Student-reported problems suspend a listing pending review. Two upheld complaints and the vendor is removed.

The one rule that protects everything

Non-negotiable

Payment never changes what Kip says. A vendor can appear in the directory, and can be surfaced when a student's question reaches the refer tier for their category — but ranking within a category is by registration status, response time and student feedback, never by fee. The answer text itself never contains a paid recommendation. The moment a student can reasonably suspect the advice was bought, the product is finished, and no referral fee is worth that.

Practically, this means vendor data lives entirely outside the answer pipeline. Kip's system prompt has no knowledge of vendors at all — it produces the referral category ("you need a MARA-registered agent"), and the application renders matching vendors alongside the answer, in a visually distinct panel. That separation is architectural, so it cannot be quietly eroded later by a product decision.

§ 15

University partners, and where their ads can go

Universities are the highest-value partner and the hardest to win. They are also the reason to be strict about advertising: an institution will not put its name on a tool that blurs advice and promotion, and neither should you.

What a university actually buys

  • Reduced support load. International student support teams answer the same 40 questions thousands of times a semester. Kip absorbs those and escalates the rest.
  • A co-branded companion. "Kip for UQ" — university branding, university-specific content injected into the knowledge base (their orientation dates, their student legal service, their accommodation office, their emergency contacts).
  • Aggregate insight, never individual. A dashboard showing what their cohort is struggling with this month — housing questions up 40% in February — built entirely from question_gaps and module_id, which contain no identities by construction (§3, §6). This is a genuinely valuable product that is also privacy-safe, which is a rare combination and worth leading with in a pitch.
  • A defensible duty-of-care story. The crisis tier means a distressed student meets 000, Lifeline and the university's own counselling service before anything else.

Where promotion is allowed to appear

How a partnership gets signed, realistically

  1. Start with oneA single university, ideally where you have a personal introduction. Nobody signs the first deal on a cold email.
  2. Enter through the international student support officeNot IT, not marketing. Support staff feel the repeated-question pain daily and will advocate internally on your behalf.
  3. Run a free semester pilotOne cohort, co-branded, with the aggregate dashboard turned on. The pilot's job is to produce a number: questions handled, support tickets avoided.
  4. Clear procurement with the document you are readingThey will ask about data residency, breach notification, encryption at rest, and where student data goes. §6, §7, §10 and §11 are those answers. Move to Sydney hosting before this conversation.
  5. Price per enrolled international student, per yearInstitutions buy per-seat annual licences; they do not buy usage-metered AI tools. Match the shape of budget they already have.
  6. Use the first deal to open the sectorAustralian universities benchmark against each other constantly. The second contract is dramatically easier than the first.

Three things not to do

  • Do not sell advertising inside answers. It is the fastest money and it ends the institutional business permanently.
  • Do not sell or share student data in any form that could identify a person. The architecture makes this mostly impossible; keep it that way, and say so publicly — it is a competitive advantage, not a constraint.

The short version, if you only remember one thing

Your 434 questions live in the code repository, version-controlled and recoverable, and are read into memory when the server starts. What students ask that Kip handles badly is recorded as a de-identified topic list with dates only and no link to any person. Everything a student actually says is encrypted before it reaches the database, with a key the database does not contain. A stolen database, a stolen backup or a stolen laptop yields nothing readable. That is the claim, and §6, §7 and §10 are the evidence for it.
